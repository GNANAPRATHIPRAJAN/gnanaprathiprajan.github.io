A Mentor Who Helps You Find Your Way

By Gnanaprathiprajan M

When I think about the kind of teacher a student needs, I think beyond lessons, examinations, and qualifications. I think of someone who notices the person behind the performance: their curiosity, confusion, strengths, and the possibilities they cannot yet see in themselves.

Read moreRead less: A Mentor Who Helps You Find Your Way

To me, the word guru carries a sense of responsibility. It suggests more than someone who knows a subject well. It suggests someone willing to help another person find direction. That is what makes mentorship meaningful: the influence a teacher can have on how a student understands both their work and themselves.

A student who is still finding their way does not always know which questions to ask. Sometimes they need an explanation. Sometimes they need someone to listen before offering advice. A good mentor should try to understand that difference, rather than assume every student needs the same kind of guidance.

I would value a mentor who shares personal experience honestly, including mistakes, uncertainty, and decisions they might now make differently. Hearing only about success can make the path ahead look unrealistically smooth. Hearing how someone faced a setback can help a student approach their own difficulties with more perspective.

Guidance also needs to be practical. How do you choose a problem worth working on? How do you respond to criticism, recognise an opportunity, or admit that you do not understand something? A mentor can explain what to try and what to avoid, while also explaining why. Advice becomes more useful when a student can understand the reasoning and apply it in a different situation.

But care does not mean constant agreement. I think a good mentor should be able to say, “This needs more work,” without making a student feel incapable. High expectations and respect can belong together. Students should have room to ask questions, disagree thoughtfully, and make mistakes without being humiliated.

I also want to understand the world beyond my immediate work. A mentor’s experience can offer perspective on professional relationships, changing opportunities, and responsibilities that come with knowledge. Still, their experience is a guide, rather than a complete map of the student’s future. The world changes, and a student needs space to develop their own judgment.

Where do we find such a mentor? I would look less at reputation alone and more at everyday actions. It might be a teacher, a research supervisor, or a thoughtful senior. Do they listen carefully? Does their feedback help? Do they acknowledge what they do not know? Do they respect the student’s interests even when those interests differ from their own?

For me, the best mentorship would gradually make me more confident in thinking and deciding for myself. I would still value the relationship, but I would not need permission for every step. That is the kind of guru I hope a growing student can find: someone who walks beside them long enough to help them discover a direction they can eventually call their own.

How Do You Hold a Plasma?

By Gnanaprathiprajan M

My interest in plasma physics and fusion begins with a question I keep coming back to: how do researchers control something so hot, restless, and difficult to hold? The more I read about fusion experiments, the more curious I become about what keeps the plasma in place.

Read moreRead less: How Do You Hold a Plasma?

Plasma contains free electrons and ions. Their electric charge allows magnetic fields to influence their motion. A tokamak combines fields that run around its ring with fields that wrap around the plasma cross section, helping charged particles follow twisting paths and confining the hot plasma. The fields do not form a perfect, solid wall; maintaining confinement remains a demanding physics problem. [1]

One detail makes this especially striking to me. ITER is designed to heat its plasma to around 150 million degrees Celsius, while its superconducting magnets operate near minus 269 degrees Celsius. These are design conditions for ITER, rather than a claim that it is already producing fusion power. Extreme heat and extreme cold belong to the same machine, separated by carefully engineered systems. [2] [1]

But magnets alone are not the whole story. Plasma can shift and change shape, so control must respond to what the plasma is actually doing. At EPFL’s TCV tokamak, diagnostic measurements feed a control system that adjusts magnetic coils and other actuators. Coils help control position and shape, while fuelling and heating systems help regulate density and temperature. I find this continuous cycle of measurement and correction as remarkable as the magnets themselves. [3]

The methods are evolving too. Researchers at EPFL and DeepMind demonstrated a controller trained through deep reinforcement learning that controlled several plasma shapes in TCV experiments. It is an example of AI contributing to a specific control problem, rather than evidence that the wider challenge of fusion energy has been solved. [4]

This is what draws me towards plasma physics and fusion research. I want to understand how researchers decide which magnetic fields to change, how they detect the plasma moving, and how quickly a correction must happen. Each explanation leaves me with another question. For now, that curiosity is the heart of my interest: how can we understand this moving, changing plasma well enough to guide it?

  2. ITER: External heating systems
  3. EPFL Swiss Plasma Center: Plasma control
  4. Degrave et al., Nature: Magnetic control of tokamak plasmas through deep reinforcement learning

Learning with AI, Thinking for Myself

By Gnanaprathiprajan M

When some of my friends in IT talk about their jobs, uncertainty enters the conversation. They wonder how their work will change, which skills will matter, and whether the roles they are preparing for will still need them.

Read moreRead less: Learning with AI, Thinking for Myself

I listen from a different position. I am a physics student and researcher, exploring questions that bring me closer to biology and computation. Programming is not something I have mastered. Yet AI has made it easier for me to approach work that once felt beyond my reach.

That is where my own questions begin.

For my friends, AI can feel like a challenge to their place at work. For me, it has opened a door. It helps me approach unfamiliar concepts, understand code, and connect ideas across subjects. Both experiences can exist at the same time. Something that makes my work easier can also make someone else anxious about their future.

The convenience is difficult to ignore. Instead of moving between search results, documentation, and explanations written for different audiences, I can ask a question in my own words. If I do not understand the answer, I can ask again. I can request a simpler explanation or connect it to physics, where I feel more comfortable.

But sometimes I wonder what happens between receiving an explanation and understanding it.

An answer can feel clear while I am reading it. Could I explain it later without opening the conversation again? If a piece of code produces a result, could I recognise a mistake? Am I learning the reasoning, or becoming familiar with how a convincing explanation sounds?

I do not think using help makes learning less meaningful. Research has always involved learning from other people and their work. What concerns me is how easily convenience might shorten the time I spend questioning an answer. Having everything in one conversation can make the search feel complete before I have checked the evidence.

There is another question I find harder to dismiss. What if the service I build my workflow around becomes too expensive, changes its limits, or is no longer available to me? Would I still have the knowledge and resources to continue?

I do not want these questions to lead me away from a useful tool. I want them to influence how I use it.

For me, a practical beginning would be to slow down at the important steps: understand the assumptions behind a calculation, read the documentation for a function, trace a small section of code, and try explaining a concept without assistance. I want to keep notes that make sense outside the chat window, including what I have verified and what I still do not understand.

My friends’ concerns about employment cannot be solved by my personal learning habits. Those concerns deserve attention from employers and policymakers too. But our different experiences have brought me to a shared question: how do we move forward with a technology while keeping a meaningful say in our own futures?

I do not have a complete answer yet. For now, I want AI to help me explore further, and I want my understanding to grow with that exploration.
